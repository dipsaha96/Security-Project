One-line summary: This project builds a small isolated virtual network, launches a classic Smurf denial-of-service attack inside it (where one attacker packet is multiplied into many packets aimed at a victim), and then demonstrates four different defenses that each shut the attack down — all measured with real packet captures.

1. What is a Smurf Attack? (the core idea)

A Smurf attack is a reflection + amplification denial-of-service attack that abuses ICMP (ping).

It relies on two tricks used together:

  1. Source-address spoofing — the attacker forges the "From" address on a ping request, writing the victim's IP address as the sender instead of their own.
  2. Directed broadcast — the attacker sends that ping not to one host, but to a network's broadcast address (e.g. 203.0.113.255), which delivers it to every host on that network at once.

The result: every host on the broadcast network ("amplifiers") receives a ping request that appears to come from the victim, so they all send their ping replies to the victim. One packet from the attacker becomes N packets hitting the victim, where N is the number of amplifier hosts. Scale N up and the victim is flooded — that is the denial of service.

Why it's called "amplification": the attacker spends little bandwidth (a few small packets) but the victim absorbs a much larger volume. In our lab the amplification factor is 6× (6 amplifier hosts). On the real 1990s Internet it could be hundreds or thousands.

2. Project Goal

  • Recreate the full Smurf attack chain safely in an isolated lab (no real networks touched).
  • Measure the amplification with packet captures at the victim.
  • Implement and verify four defenses, showing each one reduces the attack traffic to zero.
  • Produce analysis and plots comparing attack vs. defenses.

4. How the Attack Works — step by step

  1. The attacker runs smurf_attack.py, which crafts ICMP Echo Requests with a forged source IP = the victim (198.51.100.10) and a destination = the amplifier broadcast (203.0.113.255).
  2. The packet travels to the router. The router forwards the directed broadcast onto the amplifier network, delivering it to all six amplifier hosts.
  3. Each amplifier sees a ping "from the victim" and dutifully sends an Echo Reply back to the victim.
  4. The victim is hit by 6 replies for every 1 packet the attacker sent. Repeating this at speed floods the victim.

5. The Four Defenses

Each defense is enabled one at a time, the same attack is re-run, and the victim's capture is checked. In every case the reflected traffic reaching the victim drops to zero.

#DefenseRuns onHow it worksStandard
1Disable directed-broadcast forwardingRouter Router drops any packet aimed at the broadcast address (203.0.113.255), so the ping never fans out to the amplifiers. The root-cause fix. RFC 2644
2Ingress filtering (source validation)Router Router checks that a packet's source address matches the network it arrived on (reverse-path filtering + iptables). The spoofed "from victim" packet arriving on the attacker network is rejected. BCP 38 / RFC 2827
3Suppress broadcast echo repliesAmplifiers Each host sets icmp_echo_ignore_broadcasts = 1, so it simply ignores pings sent to a broadcast address — removing it from the reflection pool. §10.2
4ICMP rate limitingRouter iptables caps how many ICMP Echo Replies per second may pass. A mitigation (limits impact) rather than a cure — the reflection path still exists. §10.2
Defense strength, ranked: Defenses 1 & 2 are the strongest because they stop the attack at its two enabling conditions (broadcast + spoofing). Defense 3 removes the amplifiers. Defense 4 only softens the blow. A real network would combine them (defense in depth).

8. Key Technical Points (things the examiner may probe)

  • Why does the victim get flooded and not the attacker? Because the source IP is spoofed to the victim, so the amplifiers reply to the victim, not the true sender.
  • Why is a broadcast used? To reach many hosts with one packet — that is where the amplification comes from.
  • Why is this "reflection"? The victim never talks to the attacker; the amplifiers are innocent third parties who reflect traffic, hiding the attacker.
  • Directed-broadcast forwarding had to be explicitly enabled on the router (net.ipv4.conf.*.bc_forwarding=1) because modern Linux drops directed broadcasts by default — which is itself Defense 1 in action.
  • Isolation: the lab has no route to the internet (the router carries no default route), so the attack cannot escape — important for doing this safely and ethically.

9. Likely Q&A

Q: Is the Smurf attack still a threat today?
A: Rarely in its original form — modern routers disable directed-broadcast forwarding by default (RFC 2644) and many networks deploy ingress filtering (BCP 38). But the underlying idea — reflection & amplification — is very much alive in DNS, NTP, and memcached amplification DDoS attacks.
Q: What is the single most effective defense?
A: Disabling directed-broadcast forwarding on routers (Defense 1) kills this specific attack at the source. Ingress filtering (Defense 2) is the most important general defense because it stops source spoofing, which underlies almost all reflection attacks.
Q: How did you prove the attack and defenses actually work?
A: With packet captures (tcpdump) taken on the victim. Under attack we captured 60 reflected replies from 6 amplifiers; with each defense enabled the victim captured 0 — objective, measured evidence rather than assumption.
Q: Why Docker instead of real machines or VMs?
A: Lightweight, reproducible, and fully isolated. Nine "hosts" and three networks run on one laptop, the whole topology is code (docker-compose.yml), and it can be torn down and rebuilt identically in seconds.
Q: Why do the amplifiers respond to a broadcast ping at all?
A: By default a host answers ICMP Echo Requests, including those sent to its network's broadcast address. Defense 3 turns that behavior off.
